Strategic Summary

The architecture and the decision logic an interviewer expects.

Scope assumption.

Data point [the exact MMCI product taxonomy] is unavailable; proceeding with consumer telecom competitive intelligence across broadband, wireless, video, devices, promotions, bundles, channels, and geography. The architecture remains valid if the domain taxonomy changes.

Core recommendation

Keep the lakehouse as the system of record, expose trusted temporal facts through governed Snowflake views, and build the AI layer as a separate evidence-oriented consumption plane.

Agent recommendation

Use a deterministic LangGraph workflow with a few bounded model decisions. Prefer specialized nodes over a swarm of conversational agents.

Platform recommendation

Use FastAPI on AKS when platform control, private networking, policy, scaling, and multiple services justify Kubernetes. Re-evaluate Azure Container Apps for simpler workloads.

02

Competitive Intelligence Business Model

What the system is meant to improve, not merely what technology it uses.

Offer intelligence

Detect competitor plan launches, price changes, limited-time promotions, speed-tier changes, device offers, and eligibility conditions.

Market comparison

Compare like-for-like offers by geography, channel, customer segment, contract term, bundle dependency, and effective period.

Decision support

Answer “what changed, where, why it matters, and what action is supported by the evidence.”

Operational leverage

Reduce analyst discovery time while increasing source coverage, freshness, reproducibility, and auditability.

Canonical questions the platform should answer

  • Which competitor changed its entry broadband price in the last seven days?
  • What is the normalized 12-month customer cost after equipment, activation, auto-pay, and promotion expiry?
  • Where do we have a feature or coverage disadvantage by ZIP, DMA, or footprint?
  • Which claims are based on current primary evidence versus stale or inferred evidence?
  • What changed between two offer snapshots?
  • Which competitor actions are isolated versus broad market signals?
  • What insights have high business impact but weak evidence confidence?
  • Which product, pricing, or channel teams should receive an alert?

Critical caveat.

“Cheapest plan” is usually not a valid business fact without a normalization policy. Include taxes when available, equipment, installation, auto-pay, paperless billing, bundle requirements, promotional duration, post-promo price, contract, credits, and geographic eligibility.

Source of truth

Temporal competitive facts live in the data platform. Search indexes are derived retrieval stores, not authoritative databases.

Model boundary

The model receives only the context and tools required for the current step. It does not receive unrestricted credentials or full data access.

Delivery boundary

FastAPI exposes typed contracts. AKS provides the runtime controls. The UI never directly calls search, Snowflake, or model endpoints.

Strong interview explanation.

“The design preserves ADLS as the durable data plane, uses Databricks and Unity Catalog for transformation and governance, exposes selected Iceberg tables to Snowflake through catalog integration, and shields applications behind stable secure views. This avoids unnecessary copies while maintaining a governed presentation contract.”

Interview question: Is this really zero-copy?
Weak answer: “Yes, no data movement happens at all.”
Better answer: “The architecture avoids a second persisted business-data copy in Snowflake for the linked Iceberg tables. Queries still read files, use metadata, incur network and compute, and may create caches or temporary data. I validate compatibility, latency, and egress implications.”
Interview question: Why not let applications query the linked database directly?
Weak answer: “Secure views are only for security.”
Better answer: “Secure views also create a stable semantic contract, hide source schema churn, apply business definitions, simplify authorization, and protect API latency by constraining query patterns.”

Critical points.
  • Separate “when the source was observed” from “when the offer became effective.”
  • Preserve the source text and screenshot/document reference that supports every material claim.
  • Use a source hierarchy and contradiction workflow. Do not silently merge conflicting facts.
  • Treat parsing and normalization rules as versioned code with regression datasets.
  • Collect only from sources your organization is authorized to use. Respect licensing, access controls, contractual terms, rate limits, and legal review requirements.
Interview question: How do you detect a price change reliably?
Weak answer: “I compare the current page text with yesterday’s page.”
Better answer: “I hash canonicalized source regions for efficient detection, preserve immutable snapshots, extract structured price components, compare normalized temporal facts, and route ambiguous changes for validation. I distinguish source layout changes from business changes.”
Interview question: Why is a vector database not the system of record?
Weak answer: “Vector databases are eventually consistent.”
Better answer: “The main issue is semantics. A vector index is optimized for retrieval, not temporal truth, transactional constraints, lineage, or metric calculation. It is a derived index that can be rebuilt from governed facts and evidence.”

Evidence-First RAG for Competitive Intelligence

Retrieval must combine semantic evidence, exact business terms, structured filters, and trusted calculations.

Index document

Chunk text plus source, competitor, product, geography, channel, observed/effective dates, evidence ID, sensitivity, and quality status.

Evidence pack

A bounded, ranked set of passages and structured facts with stable IDs. It should be inspectable before generation.

Answer contract

Claims, citations, calculation references, confidence, freshness, unresolved conflicts, and a clear “insufficient evidence” outcome.

Where many implementations fail.
  • They use vector-only retrieval and miss exact plan names, acronyms, product codes, and price terms.
  • They retrieve semantically similar but geographically ineligible offers.
  • They calculate prices in the prompt instead of a deterministic tool.
  • They cite a document that is relevant to the topic but does not support the exact claim.
  • They evaluate answer style but not retrieval recall, citation entailment, or temporal correctness.
Interview question: Why hybrid search instead of vector search?
Weak answer: “Hybrid is always more accurate.”
Better answer: “Competitive intelligence contains semantic language and exact identifiers. Keyword retrieval protects exact-match recall, vector retrieval captures meaning, filters enforce business scope, and reranking improves ordering. I validate the combination on a labeled query set rather than assuming it wins for every query.”
Interview question: How do you prevent stale evidence?
Weak answer: “I sort by timestamp.”
Better answer: “I filter by effective and observed dates, retain historical queries explicitly, boost freshness only within the correct temporal scope, expose source freshness in the answer, and fail closed when a current answer lacks evidence inside the SLA.”

FastAPI for Production AI APIs

FastAPI is the application boundary, not the orchestration strategy or the scaling system.

sequenceDiagram participant UI as Client or UI participant GW as Gateway participant API as FastAPI pod participant AUTH as Entra ID / policy participant ORCH as LangGraph service participant DEP as Search, SQL, model APIs UI->>GW: HTTPS request with token GW->>API: Routed request + correlation ID API->>AUTH: Validate identity and scopes AUTH-->>API: Claims and authorization context API->>ORCH: Typed request + deadline ORCH->>DEP: Concurrent bounded I/O DEP-->>ORCH: Evidence and tool results ORCH-->>API: Structured response + trace IDs API-->>GW: Stream or JSON response GW-->>UI: Result, citations, status

Use FastAPI for

  • typed request and response contracts
  • authentication context propagation
  • timeouts, cancellation, and streaming
  • dependency injection and lifecycle resources
  • OpenAPI and service integration

Do not use it for

  • durable long-running job state
  • CPU-heavy parsing on the event loop
  • unbounded background tasks
  • global in-memory conversation state
  • secret or policy decisions inside prompts

Production companions

  • AKS, Container Apps, or App Service
  • Redis or durable state store when justified
  • queue or workflow engine for long jobs
  • OpenTelemetry and centralized logs
  • API Management for external governance

Reference implementation pattern

from contextlib import asynccontextmanager
from fastapi import FastAPI, Depends, HTTPException, Request
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field
import asyncio, httpx

class InsightRequest(BaseModel):
    question: str = Field(min_length=3, max_length=4000)
    market: str | None = None
    as_of_date: str | None = None

class InsightResponse(BaseModel):
    answer: str
    citations: list[str]
    confidence: float
    trace_id: str

@asynccontextmanager
async def lifespan(app: FastAPI):
    limits = httpx.Limits(max_connections=200, max_keepalive_connections=50)
    app.state.http = httpx.AsyncClient(timeout=20.0, limits=limits)
    app.state.llm_slots = asyncio.Semaphore(32)
    yield
    await app.state.http.aclose()

app = FastAPI(lifespan=lifespan)

@app.post("/v1/insights", response_model=InsightResponse)
async def create_insight(payload: InsightRequest, request: Request):
    try:
        async with request.app.state.llm_slots:
            async with asyncio.timeout(25):
                return await run_graph(payload, request.state.auth_context)
    except TimeoutError as exc:
        raise HTTPException(status_code=504, detail="Upstream deadline exceeded") from exc
Critical implementation points.
  • async def improves concurrency only when the dependency chain is non-blocking.
  • Use shared clients and connection pools created in application lifespan, not a new client per request.
  • Propagate an end-to-end deadline. Nested default timeouts frequently exceed the user SLA.
  • Apply concurrency limits before rate-limited model and search dependencies.
  • Handle client disconnect and cancellation so expensive downstream work does not continue unnecessarily.
  • Use Server-Sent Events or streaming only when the consumer benefits. Streaming can hide poor time-to-first-token and complicate retries.

Interview question: Does making every endpoint async improve latency?
Weak answer: “Yes, async is faster than sync.”
Better answer: “Async primarily improves concurrency for waiting workloads. A blocking SDK inside an async endpoint stalls the event loop. CPU-bound work still needs processes or a separate worker. I profile event-loop lag, pool saturation, and dependency latency.”
Interview question: How many workers should a FastAPI pod run?
Weak answer: “Two times CPU plus one.”
Better answer: “That formula is not a Kubernetes default. I start with one process per pod for an I/O-heavy AI API, set realistic requests and limits, load test, and add workers only when CPU utilization, memory duplication, and scaling behavior justify it.”
Interview question: How do you prevent a model outage from exhausting the API?
Strong answer: “Use short bounded timeouts, bulkhead semaphores, queue limits, circuit breaking where appropriate, capped retries with jitter only for retryable failures, degraded responses, and autoscaling metrics that do not amplify the dependency outage.”

Critical caveats.
  • CPU-based HPA may not represent an AI API bottleneck. Track concurrency, queue depth, dependency latency, in-flight requests, and model/search rate limits.
  • Autoscaling cannot fix a downstream quota. It can increase failures by adding callers.
  • Do not set CPU limits blindly for latency-sensitive Python services. Throttling can increase tail latency.
  • Use separate deployments for APIs and long-running jobs. A single pod should not mix user traffic with unpredictable batch work.
  • Use workload identity rather than long-lived service-principal secrets.
  • For self-hosted models, GPU scheduling, model loading time, KV cache, batching, and inference engine selection become separate architecture concerns.
Interview question: Why AKS instead of Azure Container Apps?
Weak answer: “AKS scales better.”
Better answer: “I choose AKS when I need Kubernetes APIs, custom networking or policy, specialized node pools, sidecars/operators, platform standardization, or fine-grained scheduling. For a small stateless API with event scaling and limited platform requirements, Container Apps can reduce operational overhead.”
Interview question: What metric should scale an agent API?
Weak answer: “CPU above 70 percent.”
Better answer: “CPU may be secondary because the service waits on models and search. I use in-flight concurrency, request queue time, p95 latency, queue depth for workers, and dependency quotas, with a cap that protects downstream services.”

LangChain: Integration and Agent Harness

Use LangChain to standardize model, tool, middleware, and structured-output integration. Do not let it obscure architecture.

Good fit

  • provider-neutral model wrappers
  • tool schemas and invocation
  • structured output
  • retriever integrations
  • middleware for context and guardrails

Use cautiously

  • rapidly changing convenience APIs
  • deep chains of implicit runnables
  • large callback stacks
  • provider features hidden by a lowest-common-denominator wrapper
  • serialization of complex runtime objects

Architecture rule

Keep domain logic, authorization, data contracts, and retries in your application. Framework objects should be replaceable adapters around those decisions.

Recommended layering

  policies.py        # Authorization, freshness, confidence rules
  calculations.py    # Deterministic cost and comparison logic

  snowflake.py       # Metric and fact queries

  prompts.py         # Versioned prompts

  routes.py          # FastAPI contracts
  dependencies.py    # Auth and request context

  tracing.py         # OpenTelemetry and evaluation hooks
Critical points.
  • Use provider-native structured output when available; validate again with Pydantic at the application boundary.
  • Tool descriptions are part of system behavior. Test them like prompts.
  • Wrap retrievers behind an internal interface so index technology can change.
  • Pin versions, maintain integration tests, and isolate upgrade work.
  • Avoid using framework memory as the authoritative source of user or business data.
Interview question: What does LangChain add if the model SDK already supports tools?
Weak answer: “LangChain is needed to build RAG.”
Better answer: “It is optional. It provides reusable integrations, middleware, retrievers, tool abstractions, and structured agent harnesses. For a simple workflow, the native SDK can be clearer and lower risk. I choose it when abstraction benefits exceed upgrade and observability costs.”
Interview question: How do you avoid framework lock-in?
Strong answer: “I keep domain models, tool contracts, evaluation datasets, authorization, and orchestration intent independent. Provider and framework code lives in adapters. A replacement should not change the business API or evidence model.”

LangGraph: Durable, Explicit Orchestration

LangGraph is most valuable when the workflow requires state, branching, retries, pause/resume, and inspectability.

State

Typed snapshot of question, constraints, evidence, facts, decisions, retries, and audit metadata.

Nodes

Small units that perform deterministic logic, model calls, retrieval, or controlled side effects.

Edges

Explicit routing based on state. Conditional edges are preferable to an opaque autonomous loop.

Checkpoints

Persist execution state for durable resume, human review, fault recovery, and traceability.

Where many go wrong.
  • They put all context into one ever-growing state object and persist large documents repeatedly.
  • They perform non-idempotent side effects in nodes that may replay after a retry or resume.
  • They confuse checkpoint state with long-term memory.
  • They let the LLM choose every edge when deterministic rules would be safer.
  • They omit graph version and state migration strategy.
  • They do not set recursion, retry, token, cost, or time budgets.

Node design rules

  • Read explicit state fields and return a narrow update
  • Use stable tool contracts and typed outputs
  • Record external request IDs and idempotency keys
  • Separate pure computation from side effects
  • Make retryability explicit by error type
  • Do not hide multiple model and tool calls inside an unobservable “agent” node
  • Do not persist secrets or raw tokens in checkpoints
  • Do not reuse one thread ID across users or authorization contexts
  • Do not assume replay is harmless
  • Do not allow unbounded tool loops
Interview question: LangChain agent versus LangGraph?
Weak answer: “LangGraph is for multi-agent and LangChain is for single-agent.”
Better answer: “LangChain provides higher-level agent and integration abstractions. LangGraph is the orchestration runtime for explicit state, branching, persistence, interrupts, and durable execution. A LangChain agent can run on LangGraph, and LangGraph can orchestrate deterministic or agentic nodes.”
Interview question: How do you make a graph durable?
Strong answer: “Use a production checkpointer, stable thread and run IDs, idempotent nodes, external side-effect records, graph and state schema versions, bounded retries, and a resume policy. Durability is not achieved merely by enabling persistence.”

Default design for MMCI.

Use one explicit graph with specialized nodes for intent parsing, evidence retrieval, SQL metrics, conflict resolution, synthesis, and claim validation. Suggested bounded roles

Research planner

Decomposes a complex question into evidence and metric needs. It cannot publish or change data.

Evidence retriever

Queries approved indexes with required filters and returns source-level evidence IDs.

Metric analyst

Calls predefined SQL/calculation tools. It does not generate SQL against unrestricted databases.

Conflict resolver

Applies source hierarchy and temporal rules, then flags unresolved conflicts.

Insight synthesizer

Builds a structured answer from evidence and metrics, including uncertainty.

Claim validator

Checks every material claim against cited evidence and policy before release.

Integrated vectorization

Useful when Azure-managed indexers, skillsets, chunking, projections, and embedding are a good fit. It reduces custom pipeline code.

Custom indexing

Prefer when normalization, temporal facts, custom parsing, exact chunk IDs, or multi-stage validation are central to the domain.

Blue/green index

Create a new versioned index, backfill and evaluate, then switch through an alias or application configuration.

Critical caveats.
  • Changing many index field properties requires a rebuild. Plan aliases and versioned schemas.
  • Search score is not a calibrated probability and scores from different query modes are not directly interchangeable.
  • Semantic reranking improves ordering of textual results; it does not replace correct filters or temporal modeling.
  • Approximate vector search introduces a recall/latency tradeoff. Measure against exact or labeled relevance baselines.
  • Security trimming must be enforced at query time and tested for cross-tenant or cross-group leakage.
  • Search indexes can lag the source of truth. Expose freshness and index pipeline health.
Interview question: How do BM25, vectors, RRF, and semantic ranker interact?
Weak answer: “Semantic ranker merges keyword and vector results.”
Better answer: “Keyword and vector queries execute, RRF merges their ranked lists, and semantic ranking can rerank the resulting text-rich candidates. Filters constrain eligible documents. Each stage solves a different problem.”
Interview question: One index or many indexes?
Strong answer: “I decide based on isolation, schema divergence, scale, lifecycle, query patterns, and operational ownership. A shared index with hard filters is efficient for consistent tenants; separate indexes improve isolation and independent lifecycle but increase cost and operations.”

Optimize the entire critical path, not only model choice.

Optimization hierarchy

  1. Remove unnecessary calls. One fewer model call usually beats micro-optimizing Python.
  2. Parallelize independent I/O. Retrieval, permitted SQL metrics, and metadata checks can often run concurrently.
  3. Reduce context. Better filtering and evidence compression reduce tokens, latency, and distraction.
  4. Use the smallest adequate model. Route simple extraction, classification, and validation to smaller models when measured quality permits.
  5. Cache stable artifacts. Cache embeddings, normalized queries, source metadata, and approved answers only with correct authorization and freshness keys.
  6. Control concurrency. Protect dependencies with queues, semaphores, and quotas rather than allowing a retry storm.

  • Autoscaling should include a dependency protection cap.

AI Engineer and Architect Interview Playbook

How to present the project at different levels of depth.

30-second project summary

“I designed a governed competitive-intelligence platform for Mass Markets. Authorized competitor evidence lands in ADLS and is normalized through Databricks into temporal facts governed by Unity Catalog. Curated Iceberg datasets are exposed to Snowflake through a linked read-only database and stable secure views. The AI layer indexes approved evidence in Azure AI Search and uses a bounded LangGraph workflow to combine hybrid retrieval with trusted SQL metrics. FastAPI exposes typed APIs on AKS, with workload identity, private connectivity, evaluation, tracing, and claim-level citation validation.”

Two-minute architecture narrative

  1. Business: Detect and explain competitor product, pricing, promotion, and market changes faster and with evidence.
  2. Data: Preserve immutable source evidence, normalize entities and price components, and model effective time.
  3. Governance: Use Unity Catalog and secure Snowflake views to control stable consumption contracts.
  4. Retrieval: Use Azure AI Search hybrid retrieval, metadata filters, temporal constraints, and semantic reranking.
  5. Reasoning: Use LangGraph for explicit state, deterministic branching, trusted calculations, conflict handling, and human review.
  6. Serving: Use FastAPI on AKS with private endpoints, workload identity, bounded concurrency, and end-to-end deadlines.
  7. Quality: Evaluate ingestion, retrieval, citation, tool use, safety, latency, cost, and analyst outcomes separately.

High-frequency questions

Why RAG instead of fine-tuning?
Competitive facts change frequently and need evidence, temporal filters, and access control. RAG updates knowledge without retraining. Fine-tuning may improve behavior, format, or domain style but should not be used as the source of current prices.
Why Snowflake and Azure AI Search?
Snowflake handles structured facts, governed metrics, and joins. AI Search handles lexical, vector, filtered, and semantically reranked evidence retrieval. They solve different query shapes.
How do you handle hallucinations?
Constrain the task, retrieve evidence, use deterministic tools, require structured outputs and citations, validate claims, expose uncertainty, and fail closed when evidence is insufficient. “Lower temperature” is not the primary control.
How do you design for multi-tenancy?
Propagate tenant/group identity from the gateway, authorize every data and tool call, use filter-based or physical isolation based on risk, partition caches and state, and run negative leakage tests.
How do you version the system?
Version source parser, normalization policy, data schema, index schema, embedding model, graph, prompts, tools, model deployment, and evaluation dataset. Persist these versions in traces.
What would you change at ten times traffic?
Measure bottlenecks, enforce admission control, scale stateless API and orchestration independently, use queue-backed workers, partition indexes where justified, tune search capacity, reduce model calls, route models, and coordinate quotas rather than scaling callers blindly.
How do you deploy without downtime?
Use immutable images, rolling or canary deployment, readiness gates, PDBs, backward-compatible API/data contracts, blue/green search indexes, and progressive traffic with automated rollback.
How do you handle a model provider outage?
Apply deadlines, circuit breaking, bounded retries, a tested fallback deployment or model, degraded retrieval-only responses, and clear incident telemetry. Preserve consistency of structured output and safety policy across fallbacks.
What is the hardest part?
Usually not the model call. It is normalization of ambiguous offers, temporal truth, evidence quality, authorization, and end-to-end evaluation across changing sources.
What tradeoff did you make?
A strong answer names a real constraint, alternatives, measurable consequence, and mitigation. Example: choosing AKS for platform controls while accepting operational cost, mitigated through shared platform templates and SLO ownership.

Where candidates lose credibility.
  • They describe services but not why each exists.
  • They say “agent” when the implementation is a prompt plus two tools.
  • They claim zero hallucinations, unlimited scalability, or complete vendor independence.
  • They cannot explain failure behavior, authorization, temporal truth, or evaluation.
  • They cite average latency and ignore p95/p99, saturation, and quotas.
  • They recommend Kubernetes without explaining operational ownership.
